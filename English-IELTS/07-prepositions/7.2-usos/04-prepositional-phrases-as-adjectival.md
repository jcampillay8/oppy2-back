# Prepositional Phrases as Adjectival

**Prepositional phrases as adjectivals** are prepositional phrases that function as adjectives in a sentence.

They provide additional information about a **noun, pronoun, or noun phrase**, usually by describing or identifying it.

They commonly answer questions such as:

- **Which one?**
- **What kind?**
- **Which person or thing?**
- **Whose?** *(in some contexts)*

---

## 1. Structure

A prepositional phrase used as an adjectival generally follows this structure:

> **Preposition + Object**

The object can be a **noun, pronoun, or noun phrase**.

### Example

> "The book **on the table** is mine."

- **Preposition:** `on`
- **Object:** `the table`
- **Prepositional phrase:** `on the table`
- **Function:** Adjectival
- **Modifies:** `book`

The phrase tells us:

> **Which book?** → The book on the table.

---

# 2. Adjectival Prepositional Phrases of Location

These phrases identify or describe a noun by indicating **where it is located**.

### Examples

> "The car **in the garage** is mine."

> "The house **across the street** is for sale."

> "The students **in the classroom** are studying."

### Questions

They answer:

> **Which car?** → The car in the garage.

> **Which house?** → The house across the street.

> **Which students?** → The students in the classroom.

---

# 3. Adjectival Prepositional Phrases of Description

These phrases provide additional information that helps **describe or identify a noun**.

### Examples

> "The girl **with the blue dress** is my sister."

> "The man **with the red hat** is my teacher."

> "The woman **with a beautiful voice** won the competition."

### Questions

They answer:

> **Which girl?** → The girl with the blue dress.

> **Which man?** → The man with the red hat.

> **What kind of woman?** → The woman with a beautiful voice.

---

# 4. Adjectival Prepositional Phrases of Possession or Association

Prepositional phrases can also provide information about **ownership, association, or relationship**.

### Examples

> "The student **with the laptop** is my friend."

> "The company **with offices in Chile** is expanding."

> "The house **of my grandparents** is very old."

### Questions

They can answer:

> **Which student?** → The student with the laptop.

> **Which company?** → The company with offices in Chile.

> **Whose house?** → The house of my grandparents.

---

# 5. Position in a Sentence

Adjectival prepositional phrases typically appear **after the noun they modify**.

### Example

> "The book **on the desk** is mine."

The phrase **"on the desk"** comes immediately after the noun **"book"**.

### Another Example

> "The students **in the classroom** are studying."

The phrase **"in the classroom"** comes immediately after the noun **"students"**.

---

# 6. Identifying the Modified Noun

One of the easiest ways to identify an adjectival prepositional phrase is to ask:

> **Which noun does the phrase describe or identify?**

### Example

> "The woman **in the blue car** is my manager."

The prepositional phrase is:

> **in the blue car**

Ask:

> **Which woman?**

Answer:

> The woman in the blue car.

Therefore, **"in the blue car"** is functioning as an **adjectival prepositional phrase**.

---

# 7. Adjectival vs. Adverbial

The most important distinction is **what the prepositional phrase modifies**.

### Adjectival

> "The students **in the library** are studying."

**"In the library"** modifies the noun **"students"**.

Question:

> **Which students?**

Answer:

> The students in the library.

Therefore, it is **adjectival**.

### Adverbial

> "The students studied **in the library**."

**"In the library"** modifies the verb **"studied"**.

Question:

> **Where did the students study?**

Answer:

> In the library.

Therefore, it is **adverbial**.

---

# 8. Same Phrase, Different Function

The same prepositional phrase can have different functions depending on the sentence.

### Example 1 — Adjectival

> "The man **at the door** is my teacher."

**"At the door"** modifies **"man"**.

> **Which man?** → The man at the door.

**Function:** Adjectival

### Example 2 — Adverbial

> "The man is waiting **at the door**."

**"At the door"** modifies **"is waiting"**.

> **Where is the man waiting?** → At the door.

**Function:** Adverbial

---

# 9. Identification Method

To identify a prepositional phrase functioning as an adjectival, follow these steps:

### Step 1 — Find the prepositional phrase

Look for:

> **Preposition + Object**

Example:

> "The car **in the garage** is expensive."

### Step 2 — Find the noun it describes

Ask:

> **What noun is the phrase giving information about?**

Here, the phrase describes:

> **car**

### Step 3 — Ask an adjective question

Try asking:

- **Which one?**
- **Which person?**
- **Which thing?**
- **What kind?**
- **Whose?**

### Step 4 — Confirm the function

If the phrase describes or identifies a **noun or pronoun**, it is functioning as an **adjectival**.

---

# 10. Removing the Prepositional Phrase

Adjectival prepositional phrases can sometimes be removed while leaving a grammatically complete sentence.

However, removing them may remove important identifying information.

### Example

> "The book **on the table** is mine."

Without the phrase:

> "The book is mine."

The sentence is still grammatically correct, but we no longer know **which book**.

The phrase **"on the table"** provides additional identifying information.

## Important Note

A prepositional phrase functions as an **adjectival** when it provides additional information about a **noun, pronoun, or noun phrase**.

The easiest way to recognize one is to ask:

> **Which one?**

> **What kind?**

> **Which person or thing?**

If the prepositional phrase answers one of these questions about a noun, it is functioning as an **adjectival**.

> **Key idea:** A **prepositional phrase as an adjectival** describes or identifies a noun, helping us understand **which person, place, thing, or what kind of thing** we are talking about.
