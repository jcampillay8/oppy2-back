# Adjective Clauses

An adjective clause, also called a relative clause, is a dependent clause that functions like an adjective. It provides additional information about a noun or pronoun in the main clause.

## 1. Definition

An adjective clause is a group of words that contains a subject and a verb and modifies a noun or pronoun.

Example:

- The book that I bought is interesting.

In this sentence:

- Noun: book
- Adjective clause: that I bought
- Main clause: The book is interesting.

The clause "that I bought" provides additional information about the book.

## 2. Introduction

Adjective clauses are commonly introduced by:

### Relative Pronouns

- who
- whom
- whose
- which
- that

Examples:

- The woman who lives next door is a doctor.
- The person whom I called was helpful.
- The student whose laptop broke was upset.
- The car which I bought is reliable.
- The book that you recommended was excellent.

### Relative Adverbs

- where
- when
- why

Examples:

- This is the house where I grew up.
- I remember the day when we first met.
- I understand the reason why she left.

## 3. Function

An adjective clause modifies a noun or pronoun by providing additional information about it.

Example:

- The engineer works at the company.

With an adjective clause:

- The engineer who designed the system works at the company.

The clause "who designed the system" tells us which engineer we are talking about.

## 4. Placement

Adjective clauses usually appear immediately after the noun or pronoun they modify.

Examples:

- The car that I bought is new.
- The woman who teaches English is friendly.
- The company where he works is international.

## 5. Essential Adjective Clauses

An essential adjective clause, also called a restrictive relative clause, provides information that is necessary to identify the noun.

It is **not separated by commas**.

Example:

- The book that I bought yesterday is on the table.

The clause "that I bought yesterday" identifies which book we are talking about.

Another example:

- The students who passed the exam will receive certificates.

The clause "who passed the exam" identifies which students.

## 6. Nonessential Adjective Clauses

A nonessential adjective clause, also called a nonrestrictive relative clause, provides additional information that is not necessary to identify the noun.

It is separated by commas.

Example:

- My brother, who lives in Santiago, is an engineer.

The information "who lives in Santiago" is additional information. We already know which brother we are talking about.

Another example:

- Jane, who is my best friend, is coming over.

## 7. Relative Pronouns

### Who

Used mainly for people and usually functions as the subject of the adjective clause.

Example:

- The man who called me is my manager.

### Whom

Used for people when the person is the object of the clause. It is more formal.

Example:

- The person whom I called was helpful.

In everyday English, "who" is often used instead of "whom":

- The person who I called was helpful.

### Whose

Used to indicate possession.

Example:

- The student whose laptop broke was upset.

### Which

Used mainly for things and animals.

Example:

- The computer which I bought is very fast.

### That

Can be used for people, things, or animals in essential clauses.

Examples:

- The man that called me is my manager.
- The computer that I bought is fast.

Important: "that" is generally **not used in nonessential clauses**.

Incorrect:

- My car, that is very old, needs repairs.

Correct:

- My car, which is very old, needs repairs.

## 8. Relative Adverbs

### Where

Used to refer to places.

Example:

- This is the restaurant where we had dinner.

### When

Used to refer to times or dates.

Example:

- I remember the day when we first met.

### Why

Used to refer to reasons.

Example:

- I understand the reason why she left.

## 9. Subject vs. Object Relative Pronouns

A relative pronoun can function as the **subject** or the **object** of the adjective clause.

### Subject

- The woman who works here is friendly.

"Who" is the subject of "works."

### Object

- The woman who I met yesterday is friendly.

"I" is the subject of "met," while "who" is the object.

In informal English, the object relative pronoun can sometimes be omitted:

- The woman who I met yesterday is friendly.
- The woman I met yesterday is friendly.

## 10. Essential vs. Nonessential

| Type | Purpose | Commas | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Essential | Identifies the noun | No | The students who studied passed. |
| Nonessential | Adds extra information | Yes | John, who studied hard, passed. |

## Quick Summary

**Adjective clause = Relative pronoun/adverb + subject + verb**

Its main function is to **describe or provide information about a noun or pronoun**.

Common relative words:

- **who** → people
- **whom** → people as objects
- **whose** → possession
- **which** → things/animals
- **that** → people/things/animals in essential clauses
- **where** → places
- **when** → time
- **why** → reason

Example:

- The engineer **who designed the system** won the award.

Here, "who designed the system" is an adjective clause modifying "engineer."
