# Common Mistakes with Conjunctions

Conjunctions are used to connect words, phrases, and clauses. Using them
correctly is important for creating sentences that are clear, grammatical,
and logically connected.

## 1. Comma Splices

A comma splice occurs when two independent clauses are joined only with
a comma.

Incorrect:
"I like to read books, I also enjoy watching movies."

Correct with a Coordinating Conjunction:
"I like to read books, and I also enjoy watching movies."

Correct with a Semicolon:
"I like to read books; I also enjoy watching movies."

Correct as Separate Sentences:
"I like to read books. I also enjoy watching movies."

Key Idea:

Independent Clause + Independent Clause

Use:
- Coordinating conjunction
- Semicolon
- Period


## 2. Incomplete Comparisons

When making a comparison, the two elements being compared should be clear
and logically complete.

Example:

"He is taller than his brother."

This sentence is grammatically correct in normal English.

However, when the comparison needs to emphasize the complete clause, we can use:

"He is taller than his brother is."

The second version makes the comparison more explicit.

Structure:

Subject + comparative adjective + than + comparison

Examples:

"She is more experienced than her colleague."

"She is more experienced than her colleague is."

"This car is faster than that one."

"This car is faster than that one is."

Important Note:

The word "than" is not itself a correlative conjunction. It is commonly
used to introduce the second part of a comparison.


## 3. Incorrect Subordinating Conjunction Usage

Subordinating conjunctions express specific relationships between clauses.

Choosing the wrong conjunction can change the meaning of the sentence.

Incorrect for the Intended Meaning:

"I will come with you although you're going to the party."

"Although" expresses contrast or concession.

If the intended meaning is a condition:

"I will come with you if you're going to the party."

Here:

- if → condition

Compare:

"Although you're tired, you should continue."

Meaning:

"Despite being tired..."

"If you're tired, you should rest."

Meaning:

"On the condition that you are tired..."

Key Idea:

| Conjunction | Relationship |
|-------------|--------------|
| because     | Cause |
| although    | Contrast / Concession |
| if          | Condition |
| when        | Time |
| while       | Simultaneous action / Contrast |
| unless      | Negative condition |
| after       | Sequence |
| before      | Sequence |
| until       | End point |


## 4. Misuse of Conjunctive Adverbs

Conjunctive adverbs such as:

- however
- therefore
- moreover
- consequently
- nevertheless
- furthermore

show a logical relationship between independent clauses.

They require appropriate punctuation.

Important Correction:

The following sentence is grammatically correct:

"She loves playing the piano; however, she is not very good at it."

The semicolon + conjunctive adverb + comma structure is perfectly valid.

Another Correct Version:

"She loves playing the piano. However, she is not very good at it."

Both are correct.

Common Incorrect Form:

"She loves playing the piano, however, she is not very good at it."

This creates a comma splice.

Correct:

"She loves playing the piano; however, she is not very good at it."

Or:

"She loves playing the piano. However, she is not very good at it."

Structure:

Independent Clause ; conjunctive adverb, Independent Clause.


## 5. Using the Wrong Conjunction

Each conjunction expresses a specific relationship.

Using the wrong conjunction can change the meaning or make the sentence
unnatural.

Example:

"I will go whether it's raining or it's sunny."

This sentence is understandable, but a more natural structure is:

"I will go whether it's raining or not."

Whether ... or

"Whether ... or" is commonly used to express alternative possibilities.

Examples:

"I don't know whether he will come or stay home."

"Whether you agree or disagree, you must follow the rules."

"I will go whether it rains or not."

Key Idea:

whether ... or → Alternative possibilities


## 6. Overusing Conjunctions

Using too many conjunctions can make a sentence sound repetitive, overly
complex, or difficult to follow.

Incorrect / Awkward:

"He studied and read and watched videos and practiced all day."

The repeated "and" makes the sentence unnecessarily heavy.

Better:

"He studied, read, watched videos, and practiced all day."

The list is clearer and more concise.

Another Example:

"She went to work and she finished the report and she answered emails
and she attended a meeting."

Better:

"She went to work, finished the report, answered emails, and attended
a meeting."

Key Idea:

Use conjunctions to clarify relationships, not simply to connect every
element.


## 7. Confusing "But" and "However"

"but" is a coordinating conjunction.

"however" is a conjunctive adverb.

They can express a similar contrast, but their punctuation is different.

With "But":

"I was tired, but I continued working."

With "However":

"I was tired; however, I continued working."

Or:

"I was tired. However, I continued working."

Incorrect:

"I was tired, however, I continued working."


## 8. Confusing "So" and "Therefore"

"so" is a coordinating conjunction.

"therefore" is a conjunctive adverb.

Both can express a result.

With "So":

"It was raining, so we stayed home."

With "Therefore":

"It was raining; therefore, we stayed home."

Incorrect:

"It was raining, therefore, we stayed home."

Key Idea:

- so → coordinating conjunction
- therefore → conjunctive adverb


## 9. Incorrect Correlative Conjunction Pairing

Correlative conjunctions work as specific pairs.

Common Pairs:

- either ... or
- neither ... nor
- both ... and
- not only ... but also
- whether ... or

Incorrect:

"Either you stay here nor you come with us."

Correct:

"Either you stay here or you come with us."

Incorrect:

"She is neither intelligent and hardworking."

Correct:

"She is neither intelligent nor hardworking."


## 10. Breaking Parallel Structure

Elements connected by correlative conjunctions should normally have the
same grammatical structure.

Incorrect:

"She likes both reading and to write."

The structures are different:

- reading → gerund
- to write → infinitive

Correct:

"She likes both reading and writing."

Or:

"She likes both to read and to write."

Another Example:

"He not only studies hard but also regular exercise."

Correct:

"He not only studies hard but also exercises regularly."


## 11. Confusing "Because" and "Because of"

These two structures are different.

Because + Clause:

"We stayed home because it was raining."

Structure:

because + subject + verb

Because of + Noun / Noun Phrase:

"We stayed home because of the rain."

Structure:

because of + noun / noun phrase

Incorrect:

"We stayed home because of it was raining."

Correct:

"We stayed home because it was raining."

Or:

"We stayed home because of the rain."


## 12. Confusing "Although" and "Despite"

These structures are also different.

Although + Clause:

"Although it was raining, we went outside."

Structure:

although + subject + verb

Despite + Noun / Gerund:

"Despite the rain, we went outside."

"Despite being tired, she continued working."

Structure:

despite + noun / gerund

Incorrect:

"Despite it was raining, we went outside."

Correct:

"Although it was raining, we went outside."

Or:

"Despite the rain, we went outside."


## 13. Using "And" When "Or" Is Needed

"and" indicates that two elements are included together.

"or" indicates a choice or alternative.

Compare:

"You can have coffee and tea."

Meaning:

Both are included.

"You can have coffee or tea."

Meaning:

Choose one alternative.

Key Idea:

AND → Addition / Combination
OR  → Choice / Alternative


## 14. Using "Neither ... Nor" with a Positive Meaning

"neither ... nor" is negative.

Example:

"She likes neither coffee nor tea."

Meaning:

- She doesn't like coffee.
- She doesn't like tea.

For a positive meaning, use:

"She likes both coffee and tea."

# Identification Method

When you see a conjunction, ask:

## Step 1 — What relationship does it express?

- Addition → and
- Contrast → but, although
- Choice → or
- Cause → because
- Condition → if
- Result → so, therefore

## Step 2 — What type of connector is it?

- and / but / or → Coordinating Conjunction
- because / although / if → Subordinating Conjunction
- either...or / both...and → Correlative Conjunction
- however / therefore / moreover → Conjunctive Adverb

## Step 3 — Check the punctuation

Coordinating:

Independent Clause, + conjunction + Independent Clause

Subordinating:

Dependent Clause, + Independent Clause

Correlative:

First part + element + second part + element

Conjunctive Adverb:

Independent Clause ; conjunctive adverb, Independent Clause


# Key Idea

The most common conjunction mistakes come from:

1. Incorrect punctuation
2. Choosing the wrong conjunction
3. Confusing conjunction types
4. Breaking parallel structure
5. Using incomplete or incompatible structures
6. Overusing conjunctions

Choose the conjunction according to the relationship you want to express,
then use the grammatical structure and punctuation required by that type
of connector.
