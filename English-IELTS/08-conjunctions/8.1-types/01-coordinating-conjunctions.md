# Coordinating Conjunctions

**Coordinating conjunctions** are words that connect **words, phrases, or clauses of equal grammatical importance** within a sentence.

They join similar or related elements and show the relationship between them.

The seven main coordinating conjunctions can be remembered using the acronym:

> **FANBOYS**

- **F** → `for`
- **A** → `and`
- **N** → `nor`
- **B** → `but`
- **O** → `or`
- **Y** → `yet`
- **S** → `so`

---

# 1. Types of Coordinating Conjunctions

## "And"

**"And"** indicates **addition, connection, or continuation**.

It connects similar or related elements.

### Examples

> "I like coffee **and** tea."

> "She enjoys reading, writing, **and** painting."

> "He finished his work, **and** he went home."

### Key Idea

> **AND → Addition / Connection**

---

## "But"

**"But"** indicates **contrast or opposition** between two ideas.

### Examples

> "I wanted to go, **but** I was too tired."

> "She studied hard, **but** she failed the test."

> "He is young, **but** he is very experienced."

### Key Idea

> **BUT → Contrast**

---

## "Or"

**"Or"** presents a **choice, alternative, or possibility**.

### Examples

> "Do you want coffee **or** tea?"

> "You can stay here **or** come with us."

> "We can go by bus, **or** we can take a taxi."

### Key Idea

> **OR → Choice / Alternative**

---

## "Nor"

**"Nor"** expresses **negative coordination**.

It is commonly used after a negative element such as **neither** or **not**.

### Examples

> "He **neither** understood the question **nor** knew how to answer it."

> "She doesn't like coffee, **nor** does she like tea."

### Key Idea

> **NOR → Negative alternative**

### Common Structure

> **Neither ... nor ...**

Example:

> "She likes **neither** coffee **nor** tea."

---

## "For"

**"For"** can introduce a **reason or explanation** for the preceding statement.

This use is relatively formal and is less common in everyday conversation.

### Examples

> "She stayed home, **for** she was feeling sick."

> "He went to bed early, **for** he had to wake up at six."

### Key Idea

> **FOR → Reason / Explanation**

### Important Note

In this context, **"for"** is a coordinating conjunction, not a preposition.

Compare:

> "I bought this gift **for** my friend."  
> → Preposition

> "I stayed home, **for** I was tired."  
> → Coordinating conjunction

---

## "Yet"

**"Yet"** introduces a **contrast or unexpected result**.

It is similar to **"but"**, but often emphasizes that something is surprising or unexpected.

### Examples

> "He failed the test, **yet** he didn't give up."

> "She is very young, **yet** she is highly experienced."

### Key Idea

> **YET → Contrast / Unexpected result**

---

## "So"

**"So"** indicates a **result, consequence, or outcome**.

### Examples

> "He studied hard, **so** he passed the exam."

> "It was raining, **so** we stayed home."

> "She was tired, **so** she went to bed."

### Key Idea

> **SO → Result / Consequence**

---

# 2. FANBOYS Quick Reference

| **Conjunction** | **Meaning / Relationship** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|
| `for` | Reason / Explanation | "I left, **for** I was tired." |
| `and` | Addition | "She sings **and** dances." |
| `nor` | Negative alternative | "Neither John **nor** Mary came." |
| `but` | Contrast | "I tried, **but** I failed." |
| `or` | Choice / Alternative | "Tea **or** coffee?" |
| `yet` | Contrast / Unexpected result | "It was difficult, **yet** he succeeded." |
| `so` | Result / Consequence | "It was late, **so** we left." |

---

# 3. What Can Coordinating Conjunctions Connect?

Coordinating conjunctions can connect elements with **equal grammatical importance**.

They can connect:

- Words
- Phrases
- Independent clauses

---

## Words

> "I like coffee **and** tea."

The conjunction connects two nouns:

> `coffee + and + tea`

---

## Phrases

> "She went **to the supermarket** and **to the pharmacy**."

The conjunction connects two prepositional phrases.

> `to the supermarket + and + to the pharmacy`

---

## Independent Clauses

> "She studied for the test, **but** she forgot her textbook."

The conjunction connects two independent clauses:

> `She studied for the test`

+

> `she forgot her textbook`

---

# 4. Coordinating Conjunctions and Compound Sentences

A **compound sentence** contains two or more **independent clauses** connected by a coordinating conjunction.

### Structure

> **Independent Clause + , + Coordinating Conjunction + Independent Clause**

### Example

> "She studied for the test, **but** she forgot her textbook."

First independent clause:

> "She studied for the test."

Second independent clause:

> "She forgot her textbook."

Combined:

> "She studied for the test, **but** she forgot her textbook."

---

# 5. Commas with Coordinating Conjunctions

When a coordinating conjunction connects **two independent clauses**, a comma is generally placed before the conjunction.

### Correct

> "I finished my work, **so** I went home."

> "She wanted to go, **but** she was too tired."

> "He studied hard, **and** he passed the exam."

### Structure

```text
Independent Clause, + FANBOYS + Independent Clause
