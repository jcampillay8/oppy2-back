# Subordinating Conjunctions

**Subordinating conjunctions** are words that introduce a **dependent clause** and establish a relationship between that clause and an **independent clause**.

They show relationships such as:

- Cause and effect
- Time
- Condition
- Contrast
- Concession
- Purpose

Unlike coordinating conjunctions, subordinating conjunctions create a relationship in which **one clause depends on the other**.

---

# 1. Independent vs. Dependent Clauses

Before understanding subordinating conjunctions, it is important to distinguish between **independent** and **dependent clauses**.

## Independent Clause

An **independent clause** can stand alone as a complete sentence.

> "I went to bed early."

It contains a complete idea.

## Dependent Clause

A **dependent clause** cannot normally stand alone as a complete sentence.

> "Because I was tired."

This leaves the reader expecting more information.

When combined:

> "I went to bed early **because I was tired**."

- **Independent clause:** `I went to bed early`
- **Subordinating conjunction:** `because`
- **Dependent clause:** `because I was tired`

---

# 2. Common Subordinating Conjunctions

Some common subordinating conjunctions include:

- `because`
- `although`
- `though`
- `if`
- `when`
- `since`
- `while`
- `after`
- `before`
- `until`
- `unless`
- `whereas`
- `as`
- `once`

---

# 3. "Because"

**"Because"** introduces a **cause or reason**.

### Examples

> "He couldn't attend the party **because** he had to work."

> "**Because** it was raining, we stayed home."

### Question

> **Why?**

> "Why did he stay home?"

> "Because it was raining."

### Key Idea

> **BECAUSE → Cause / Reason**

---

# 4. "Although" / "Though"

**"Although"** and **"though"** introduce a **contrast or concession**.

They show that something happens despite another fact or situation.

### Examples

> "**Although** it was raining, they went for a walk."

> "They went for a walk **although** it was raining."

> "**Though** he was tired, he continued working."

### Key Idea

> **ALTHOUGH / THOUGH → Contrast / Concession**

---

# 5. "If"

**"If"** introduces a **condition**.

It is especially common in conditional sentences.

### Examples

> "**If** it rains, we will stay indoors."

> "We will stay indoors **if** it rains."

> "**If** you study, you will improve."

### Key Idea

> **IF → Condition**

---

# 6. "When"

**"When"** introduces a **time relationship**.

It indicates when an action or event occurs.

### Examples

> "I will call you **when** I arrive at the airport."

> "**When** she arrived, everyone was waiting."

### Key Idea

> **WHEN → Time**

---

# 7. "Since"

**"Since"** can introduce either a **cause** or a **starting point in time**, depending on the context.

### Cause

> "**Since** it was her birthday, we threw a surprise party."

Meaning:

> **Because** it was her birthday...

### Time

> "I have lived here **since** I was a child."

Meaning:

> From the time I was a child until now.

### Key Idea

> **SINCE → Cause OR Starting point in time**

---

# 8. "While"

**"While"** indicates that two actions or situations occur **at the same time**.

It can also introduce a contrast.

### Simultaneous Actions

> "**While** I was cooking, she set the table."

> "He listened to music **while** he was studying."

### Contrast

> "**While** I understand your point, I disagree."

### Key Idea

> **WHILE → Simultaneous action OR Contrast**

---

# 9. "After"

**"After"** indicates that one action or event happens **later than another**.

### Examples

> "**After** she finished her meal, she went for a walk."

> "She went for a walk **after** she finished her meal."

### Key Idea

> **AFTER → Later than**

---

# 10. "Before"

**"Before"** indicates that one action or event happens **earlier than another**.

### Examples

> "Please finish your homework **before** you play video games."

> "**Before** you leave, please turn off the lights."

### Key Idea

> **BEFORE → Earlier than**

---

# 11. "Until"

**"Until"** indicates that an action or situation continues **up to a specific time or event**.

### Examples

> "They will wait at the station **until** the train arrives."

> "I will stay here **until** you come back."

### Key Idea

> **UNTIL → Up to a specific time or event**

---

# 12. "Unless"

**"Unless"** introduces a condition and generally means:

> **if not**

### Examples

> "We will go outside **unless** it rains."

Meaning:

> "We will go outside **if it does not rain**."

> "You won't pass the exam **unless** you study."

Meaning:

> "You won't pass the exam **if you don't study**."

### Key Idea

> **UNLESS → If not**

---

# 13. "Whereas"

**"Whereas"** introduces a contrast between two situations, people, or ideas.

### Examples

> "John prefers working in the office, **whereas** Mary prefers working from home."

> "**Whereas** the first option is cheaper, the second option is more reliable."

### Key Idea

> **WHEREAS → Contrast**

---

# 14. "Once"

**"Once"** indicates that something happens **after a particular condition or event has occurred**.

### Examples

> "**Once** you finish your work, you can leave."

> "We can start **once** everyone arrives."

### Key Idea

> **ONCE → After / When a condition is fulfilled**

---

# 15. Placement

Subordinating conjunctions can appear at the **beginning or middle of a sentence**.

## Dependent Clause First

> "**Because I was tired**, I went to bed early."

The dependent clause comes first.

## Independent Clause First

> "I went to bed early **because I was tired**."

The dependent clause comes second.

Both structures are correct.

---

# 16. Commas with Subordinating Conjunctions

When the **dependent clause comes before the independent clause**, a comma is generally used.

### Correct

> "**After he finished his homework,** he played video games."

> "**Because it was raining,** we stayed home."

> "**If you study hard,** you will pass the exam."

### When the Independent Clause Comes First

A comma is generally **not required** when the dependent clause comes after the independent clause.

> "He played video games **after he finished his homework**."

> "We stayed home **because it was raining**."

> "You will pass the exam **if you study hard**."

---

# 17. Types of Relationships

Subordinating conjunctions can express different relationships between clauses.

| **Relationship** | **Common Conjunctions** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause** | `because`, `since` | "I stayed home **because** I was sick." |
| **Contrast** | `although`, `though`, `whereas`, `while` | "**Although** it was late, he continued." |
| **Condition** | `if`, `unless` | "**If** it rains, we will stay home." |
| **Time** | `when`, `while`, `after`, `before`, `until`, `once` | "Call me **when** you arrive." |
| **Concession** | `although`, `though` | "**Although** it was difficult, she continued." |

# 19. Coordinating vs. Subordinating Conjunctions

It is important to understand the difference between **coordinating** and **subordinating conjunctions**.

### Coordinating Conjunction

Connects elements of **equal grammatical importance**.

> "I was tired, **but** I continued working."

Both clauses can stand independently:

> "I was tired."

> "I continued working."

### Subordinating Conjunction

Connects an **independent clause** with a **dependent clause**.

> "**Although** I was tired, I continued working."

The clause:

> "Although I was tired"

cannot normally stand alone as a complete sentence.

### Comparison

| **Coordinating** | **Subordinating** |
|---|---|
| `and` | `because` |
| `but` | `although` |
| `or` | `if` |
| `nor` | `when` |
| `for` | `while` |
| `yet` | `after` |
| `so` | `before` |
| Equal clauses | Dependent + independent clause |

# 20. Identification Method

To identify a subordinating conjunction, follow these steps:

### Step 1 — Find the conjunction

Look for words such as:

> `because`, `although`, `if`, `when`, `while`, `after`, `before`, `until`, `unless`

### Step 2 — Identify the clause it introduces

Example:

> "**Because I was tired**, I went to bed."

The conjunction is:

> `because`

The dependent clause is:

> `because I was tired`

### Step 3 — Check whether the clause can stand alone

> "Because I was tired."

This does not express a complete idea by itself.

Therefore, it is a **dependent clause**.

### Step 4 — Find the independent clause

> "I went to bed."

This can stand alone.

Therefore:

> **Dependent clause + Independent clause**

# Common Mistakes

## Mistake 1 — Creating a sentence fragment

❌ "Because I was tired."

This is a dependent clause, not a complete sentence.

✅ "I went to bed because I was tired."

---

## Mistake 2 — Forgetting the comma

When the dependent clause comes first:

❌ "Because I was tired I went to bed."

✅ "Because I was tired, I went to bed."

---

## Mistake 3 — Confusing "because" and "because of"

**Because** is followed by a **clause**.

> "We stayed home **because it was raining**."

**Because of** is followed by a **noun or noun phrase**.

> "We stayed home **because of the rain**."

### Structure

> **because + subject + verb**

> **because of + noun / noun phrase**

---

## Mistake 4 — Confusing "although" and "despite"

**Although** is followed by a **clause**.

> "**Although it was raining**, we went outside."

**Despite** is followed by a **noun, pronoun, or gerund**.

> "**Despite the rain**, we went outside."

---

# Important Note

Subordinating conjunctions establish a **hierarchy between ideas**.

They show that one clause is dependent on another and help express relationships such as:

> **CAUSE → because**

> **CONTRAST → although / though**

> **CONDITION → if / unless**

> **TIME → when / while / after / before / until**

> **CONCESSION → although / though**

> **Key idea:** A **subordinating conjunction** introduces a dependent clause and connects it to an independent clause, showing how the two ideas are logically related.
